# ROUND 3 — KPI Pressure Test (12 questions)

**19. "Calculate the margin yourself, right now."**
Profit ÷ Revenue × 100 = €2,733,273.35 ÷ €7,765,475.38 × 100 = 35.20%.

**20. "Why isn't revenue enough?"**
Because it doesn't show efficiency — Laptop leads revenue but has the
lowest margin (32.40%); revenue alone would have hidden that.

**21. "If revenue is increasing, why should management care about
profit?"**
Because revenue growth with flat or declining margin means the business
is generating more activity without proportionally more value retained —
profit is the number that actually reflects what's kept.

**22. "Why does AOV matter?"**
It separates market/segment scale from transaction size — Netherlands
isn't the revenue leader but has the highest AOV, a distinct signal
revenue alone wouldn't surface.

**23. Which KPI would you put at the top of a dashboard?**
Total Revenue and Profit Margin % side by side — scale and efficiency
together, not either alone.

**24. What's the formula for AOV in this project?**
Total Revenue ÷ Total Orders = €7,765,475.38 ÷ 9,985 = €777.71.

**25. What's the formula for Profit?**
Revenue − Cost, summed across all orders = €2,733,273.35.

**26. Why track Quantity separately from Revenue?**
Quantity shows unit volume; Revenue shows value — a product can move more
units at a lower price point, which Quantity alone would show and Revenue
alone would hide.

**27. How do you know these KPI definitions are consistent everywhere?**
Because I defined each formula once and reused it identically in Python,
SQL, and Excel, and the 18-metric cross-check confirmed they produce
identical results.

**28. What would make you distrust a KPI number?**
If it didn't reconcile across the three independent tools — that's
exactly the check that caught the one real Excel bug in this project.

**29. Orders vs. Revenue — which matters more?**
Neither alone — Orders shows volume, Revenue shows value; a business
needs both to know whether growth is coming from more transactions or
bigger ones.

**30. What's the single riskiest KPI to over-interpret in this project?**
The 99.88% repeat rate — numerically solid but interpretively limited by
its data-generation origin.

# ROUND 7 — SQL Live Interview (12 questions, using actual project SQL)

**61. Why did you use a CTE in the customer analysis?**
`06_customer_analysis.sql`'s `customer_orders` CTE aggregates order count/
revenue/profit per customer once, then reuses it for the repeat-count,
revenue distribution, and top-10 ranking — avoiding three separate
`GROUP BY` passes over the same logic.

**62. Why was the JOIN in this project justified rather than artificial?**
`09_advanced_analysis.sql` joins the fact table to a derived
`product_benchmark` table (per-product `AVG(Revenue)`) to answer "how many
orders per product beat their own product's average" — a genuine two-step
question a single `GROUP BY` can't answer. I explicitly did NOT build
`dim_product`/`dim_customer` tables since Product→Category and
Customer→Segment are already 1:1 in the fact table.

**63. "What happens if you group at the wrong grain?"**
E.g., grouping by Product without also grouping by Country when computing
the cross-dimensional matrix would collapse the Product×Country cells
into product-only totals — silently losing the finding that Laptop×
Germany is the strongest specific combination.

**64. Why use RANK instead of ROW_NUMBER for product ranking?**
RANK lets tied values share a rank (relevant if two products had identical
revenue); ROW_NUMBER would arbitrarily break the tie — RANK better
reflects "these two are equally ranked" if that ever occurred.

**65. Why DENSE_RANK for the per-country product ranking specifically?**
To avoid rank gaps after ties in a small 5-product list per country —
DENSE_RANK keeps consecutive ranks (1,1,2) rather than skipping (1,1,3).

**66. How would you find the top product by profit in SQL?**
`SELECT Product, SUM(Profit) AS profit FROM sales GROUP BY Product ORDER
BY profit DESC LIMIT 1;` — returns Laptop.

**67. How would you calculate country revenue in SQL?**
`SELECT Country, SUM(Revenue) AS revenue FROM sales GROUP BY Country
ORDER BY revenue DESC;` — exactly `05_market_analysis.sql`'s approach.

**68. How would you detect a duplicate Order_ID in SQL?**
`SELECT Order_ID, COUNT(*) FROM sales GROUP BY Order_ID HAVING COUNT(*) >
1;` — used in `01_data_setup.sql`'s validation; returns 0 rows.

**69. Show me the CASE statement you used and explain it.**
Order-level margin bands: `CASE WHEN (Profit*100.0/Revenue) >= 40 THEN
'High Margin' WHEN >= 25 THEN 'Medium Margin' ELSE 'Low Margin' END` —
classifies orders into profitability tiers without a separate lookup
table.

**70. What's a subquery example from this project, and what does it
answer?**
"Products above average product revenue": comparing each product's
`SUM(Revenue)` against `AVG()` of all products' summed revenue — answers
which products outperform a typical product in the lineup (only Laptop
qualifies).

**71. Why SQLite instead of a "real" database engine?**
Zero-install, single-file, most portable option for anyone cloning the
repo to reproduce it — appropriate for this project's scale (9,985 rows).

**72. How did you validate the SQL layer wasn't hardcoded?**
Every query computes from `SUM`/`GROUP BY`/window functions against the
live table — no literal result values appear anywhere in the `.sql`
files; I verified this by re-reading the query text, not just trusting
the output.

# ROUND 8 — Python / Pandas Live Interview (10 questions)

**73. Why Pandas specifically?**
Flexible groupby/aggregation syntax that made iterating on KPI definitions
fast, plus native support for the EDA (distributions, outliers,
correlation) that's awkward in SQL/Excel.

**74. Why NumPy, if Pandas already handles most of it?**
Used within the generation script's random-distribution logic and
lightweight numeric operations in the notebook — not a separate major
dependency, just Pandas' underlying numeric layer.

**75. "Why not do everything in SQL?"**
SQL doesn't naturally support exploratory visualization or statistical
checks like IQR outlier detection or Pearson correlation — Python covers
that gap; SQL still independently verifies the KPI numbers.

**76. "Why not do everything in Excel?"**
Excel isn't suited to iterative exploratory analysis or statistical
checks at the same speed as pandas — but it demonstrates business-user
accessibility that Python doesn't.

**77. How did you load and validate the data in Python?**
`pd.read_csv()` on the clean CSV, then independently re-ran 14 of the same
validation checks (missing values, duplicate Order_ID, Product→Category
consistency, Revenue/Profit formula checks) rather than trusting the
cleaning script blindly.

**78. How did you do outlier analysis?**
IQR method on Revenue, Profit, Quantity, Unit_Price. Unit_Price flagged
the most (19.55%), explained as expected given 5 differently-priced
products under one dataset-wide bound — not a data error.

**79. How did you do correlation analysis, and what's the caveat?**
Pearson correlation across the 5 numeric fields; Revenue–Cost (0.996) and
Revenue–Profit (0.982) are high but substantially mechanical, since Cost
and Profit are formula-derived from Revenue.

**80. How did you build the KPI table in pandas?**
`df.groupby('Product').agg(Revenue=('Revenue','sum'), Profit=
('Profit','sum'), ...)`, then derived Margin%, Contribution%, and Rank
columns — the exact pattern reused for Country and Customer_Segment.

**81. How did you handle time-based grouping?**
Extracted Year/Month/Quarter/Year_Month from Order_Date, then grouped for
the YoY and monthly-CV calculations.

**82. Did the notebook actually execute cleanly?**
Yes — verified 0 errors across all 33 code cells on a fresh kernel
execution via `jupyter nbconvert --execute`.

# ROUND 11 — Validation / Trust Test (10 questions)

**102. "Why should I trust your numbers?"**
Because every core metric was computed independently in three separate
tools from the same source data and matched exactly — and when a real
discrepancy did appear (the Excel bug), I found it, fixed the root cause,
and re-verified, rather than reporting a number I hadn't actually checked.

**103. "What exactly does 18/18 mean?"**
18 specific metrics — totals, product/market breakdowns, YoY figures, the
strongest cross-dimensional combination — each computed independently in
Python, SQL, and Excel, all matching exactly.

**104. "Which tools were compared?"**
Python (pandas), SQL (SQLite), and Excel (live formulas). Power BI's DAX
logic was separately verified via Python reproduction, not via Power BI's
own engine.

**105. "Why not just trust Python?"**
Because a single implementation can contain an undetected logic error —
independent recomputation in a second and third tool is the check that
actually catches it.

**106. "What if SQL and Excel disagree?"**
That's exactly what happened once (Excel vs. the others) — I investigate
the formula/query logic in both, find the actual error, fix it, and
re-run the full comparison rather than assuming either is automatically
correct.

**107. "How did you discover the Excel bug?"**
By directly inspecting the generated workbook's cell formulas during
validation, not by trusting the displayed numbers.

**108. "How did you know the final correction was correct?"**
I re-ran the cross-tool comparison after the fix and confirmed the
corrected Excel value matched Python and SQL exactly.

**109. "What's the difference between reproducibility and validation?"**
Reproducibility means the same script/process produces the same output
every time (e.g., the fixed-seed data generation is byte-identical on
rerun); validation means independently confirming the *result* is
correct by recomputing it a different way. This project demonstrates
both, but they're different claims.

**110. "Could 18/18 matching still be wrong?"**
Yes, in theory, if all three tools shared the same underlying logic error
— which is why I also independently re-verify formulas/queries by
inspection, not just by comparing final numbers.

**111. What's the strongest single piece of evidence of your rigor?**
The Excel bug: I didn't just claim validation, I found a real
discrepancy, root-caused it, fixed it, and re-verified — that's a
concrete, checkable story, not an assertion.

# ROUND 13 — Data Quality / Failure Scenarios (8 scenarios)

**Scenario 1 — SQL and Excel numbers differ.**
Situation: a KPI value doesn't match between the two tools.
Questions to Ask: which formula/query computed each value? Same date
range and filters?
Investigation: inspect both the SQL query and the Excel formula logic
directly, not just the outputs.
Likely Causes: a stale cell reference, a different rounding step, or (as
actually happened) a hardcoded/fixed-position assumption in one tool.
Decision: fix the root cause in the incorrect tool, not force-match the
number.
Validation: re-run the full cross-tool comparison after the fix.

**Scenario 2 — Revenue suddenly doubles.**
Situation: a KPI output looks implausibly high.
Questions to Ask: did the source file change? Was a filter dropped? Is
there a duplicate-row issue?
Investigation: re-run the row-count and duplicate-Order_ID checks against
the current source file.
Likely Causes: accidental duplicate rows, or a JOIN introduced a
fan-out (each fact row multiplied).
Decision: do not report the number until the cause is identified.
Validation: confirm row count and totals match the known-good baseline.

**Scenario 3 — Duplicate orders appear.**
Situation: the same Order_ID shows up more than once.
Questions to Ask: is this the source file or a downstream JOIN artifact?
Investigation: check `COUNT(*) > 1 GROUP BY Order_ID` against the raw
source, then against any intermediate query/table.
Likely Causes: a JOIN without a unique key on one side.
Decision: fix the JOIN condition or dedupe at the correct stage; never
just filter results after the fact without understanding why.
Validation: re-run the duplicate check post-fix.

**Scenario 4 — Margin becomes negative.**
Situation: Profit Margin % shows a negative value somewhere.
Questions to Ask: is Cost exceeding Revenue for specific rows? Is this a
real (simulated) scenario or a calculation error?
Investigation: filter to the specific rows where Profit < 0 and inspect
Quantity/Unit_Price/Cost directly.
Likely Causes: a data-entry/generation error, or a genuine loss-making
order if the business context allows for one (not the case in this
project's design, since normal profitability was intentionally kept
positive).
Decision: investigate before reporting — don't assume it's either "fine"
or "broken" without checking.
Validation: confirm against the Profit = Revenue − Cost formula check.

**Scenario 5 — One country disappears from the output.**
Situation: a report only shows 3 of 4 countries.
Questions to Ask: is this a filter issue, a JOIN issue, or does that
country genuinely have zero matching rows under current filters?
Investigation: check the raw distinct Country values in the source
table.
Likely Causes: an inner JOIN dropping unmatched rows, or an incorrect
WHERE filter.
Decision: fix the query logic; don't manually re-add the missing country.
Validation: confirm all 4 countries reappear with the corrected query.

**Scenario 6 — Customer count changes dramatically.**
Situation: unique customer count differs from the expected 850.
Questions to Ask: did the source file change? Is this counting
Customer_ID correctly (distinct vs. total rows)?
Investigation: re-run `nunique(Customer_ID)` against the current file.
Likely Causes: confusing order count with customer count, or a source
file mismatch.
Decision: verify against the canonical clean CSV before reporting.
Validation: cross-check the count in a second tool.

**Scenario 7 — Dashboard KPI differs from Python.**
Situation: (hypothetically, if Power BI were built) a DAX measure shows a
different number than the Python calculation.
Questions to Ask: does the DAX measure use the same filter context/logic
as the Python groupby?
Investigation: compare the DAX formula logic line-by-line against the
Python calculation (the same review process already used to verify DAX
logic without execution).
Likely Causes: a missing `ALL()`/`ALLSELECTED()` in a DAX measure causing
unintended filter propagation.
Decision: correct the DAX measure; re-verify against Python.
Validation: re-run the reference comparison.

**Scenario 8 — A stakeholder challenges the recommendation.**
Situation: a stakeholder disputes the "track margin alongside revenue"
recommendation.
Questions to Ask: what's their specific concern — the data, the
interpretation, or the practicality of the recommendation?
Investigation: revisit the underlying numbers with them directly (Laptop
margin vs. revenue) rather than defending the recommendation abstractly.
Likely Causes: a legitimate business constraint I don't have visibility
into, or a misunderstanding of what the data shows.
Decision: incorporate their context if it's a real constraint; hold the
data-based finding if it's a misunderstanding — data doesn't get
overridden by disagreement, but recommendations can be adjusted.
Validation: none needed — this is a communication resolution, not a data
issue.

# ROUND 16 — Rapid-Fire Concepts (30 questions)

1. KPI? A defined, reused business metric — e.g., Revenue, Margin.
2. AOV? Average Order Value = Revenue ÷ Orders.
3. Margin? Profit ÷ Revenue × 100.
4. YoY? Year-over-year percentage change.
5. CV? Coefficient of variation — std ÷ mean, as a %.
6. Aggregation? Collapsing detail rows into group-level totals.
7. Grain? The level of detail one row represents — here, one order.
8. EDA? Exploratory data analysis before formal KPI work.
9. Outlier? A value unusually far from the rest of a distribution.
10. Correlation? A statistical measure of linear relationship strength.
11. CTE? A named, reusable subquery (`WITH ... AS`).
12. Subquery? A query nested inside another query.
13. JOIN? Combining rows from two tables on a shared key.
14. Window function? A calculation across related rows without collapsing
    them.
15. RANK? Assigns rank, leaving gaps after ties.
16. DENSE_RANK? Like RANK but no gaps after ties.
17. ROW_NUMBER? Assigns a unique sequential number per partition.
18. DAX? Power BI's measure formula language (specification only here).
19. Power Query? Power BI's data-import/transform layer (specification
    only).
20. Data model? The table/relationship structure underlying analysis.
21. Cross-tool validation? Independently recomputing a metric in multiple
    tools and confirming a match.
22. Reproducibility? Same process, same output, every time.
23. Synthetic data? Artificially generated, not real, data — disclosed
    throughout this project.
24. Causality? A cause-and-effect relationship — not established
    anywhere in this project's findings.
25. Contribution %? A category's share of a total metric.
26. Segmentation? Splitting data into meaningful groups (e.g., Consumer/
    Business).
27. Star schema? A fact table surrounded by dimension tables — evaluated
    and mostly rejected here since attributes are already 1:1 in the
    fact table.
28. Validation vs. reproducibility? Reproducibility = same process gives
    same output; validation = independent recomputation confirms
    correctness.
29. Data cleaning? Detecting and correcting data-quality issues before
    analysis.
30. Feature engineering? Deriving new analytical fields (e.g., Year,
    Quarter) from existing ones.

# ROUND 24 — Overclaim Detection

| Interviewer Claim | Safe Response | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Your project increased revenue." | "No — no real revenue was affected; this is a synthetic, non-deployed analysis." | No business impact was measured or implemented |
| "Your analysis improved retention." | "No — the repeat-rate figure is a data-generation artifact, not a measured or improved outcome." | Retention was never actually measured |
| "You built Power BI." | "No — I designed a complete specification; the `.pbix` was never built, since Power BI Desktop wasn't available." | No `.pbix` exists |
| "You deployed the solution." | "No — nothing here was deployed anywhere; it's a self-contained portfolio project." | No deployment occurred |
| "You proved there is no seasonality." | "No material seasonality was identified in this dataset — that's not a general proof about real retail demand." | "Proved" overstates a single-dataset finding |
| "Germany is definitely the best market." | "It leads revenue and profit; Netherlands leads AOV — 'best' depends on the metric." | Single-word ranking hides the AOV finding |
| "Laptop is the most profitable product." | "It has the highest absolute profit, but the lowest margin — both are true and matter for different decisions." | "Most profitable" is ambiguous between absolute and %-based |
| "99.88% proves customer loyalty." | "It's a data-generation artifact from reusing 850 customer IDs across ~10,000 orders — not measured loyalty." | Mechanism, not behavior, produced the number |
| "Your recommendations saved money." | "No — they're evidence-based recommendations, never implemented or tested against a real outcome." | No cost saving was measured or realized |

# Final Interview Strategy

**Lead with the business problem**, not the tool list — recruiters
remember "multi-country retailer, revenue vs. profit" before they
remember "pandas."

**Answer directly first, then elaborate** — give the number or the
one-line answer, then add evidence and interpretation only as needed or
requested.

**Use evidence, not assertion** — "18/18 metrics matched" is stronger than
"I validated thoroughly"; the Excel bug story is stronger than "I'm
detail-oriented."

**Explain interpretation, not just facts** — "Laptop leads revenue" is a
fact; "which means scale and margin aren't the same story here" is the
interpretation that shows judgment.

**State limitations when they're relevant, not defensively** — volunteer
the synthetic-data disclosure and the Power BI status before being caught
out on either.

**Don't become defensive about synthetic data** — it's disclosed
everywhere in the project; treat it as a design choice, not a weakness to
hide.

**Never pretend Power BI was built** — this is the single easiest claim
in the whole project to catch as false; always say "specification."

**Never convert correlation or a descriptive ranking into causality** —
"Germany leads revenue" stops there unless asked, and then the honest
answer is "I don't have evidence for why."

**Use 18/18 validation as a strength, not a footnote** — it's the
strongest, most defensible claim in the project; lead with it when asked
"why should I trust you."

**Use the Excel bug as evidence of ownership** — finding and fixing a real
error is more convincing than claiming a flawless process.

**Demonstrate judgment, not tool-count** — the goal is never "I used four
tools," it's "I used the right tool for each question, and I can defend
every number that came out of any of them."
